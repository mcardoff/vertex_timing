The model is picking pileup

100 Z+jets events where a candidate within 60 ps existed and Deep Sets chose a different one. In 74 of them the chosen cluster contains exactly zero hard-scatter track pT. It is not mis-ranking close calls — it is selecting pure pileup, and usually the biggest one.

The mechanism

This is the ΣpT bias that WAVeS and TRKPTZ have, reproduced by a learned model. In Z+jets the hard-scatter vertex contributes few forward tracks, so the largest, best-measured, most-timed cluster in the event is usually a pileup vertex. The model has learned "big and well-measured", which is right on VBF and wrong here.

The right answer is almost always the runner-up

For each recoverable failure, where did the correct cluster sit in TRKPTZ’s own ranking? Rank 0 is the one it chose, so rank 1 is the cluster it passed over first.

87% of the misses are in the top three

Over half the time — 52.0% — the correct cluster is the very next one down the list, and 87.0% sit in the top three. Not one recoverable failure in 11,225 has its answer beyond rank 9.

Two controls before reading anything into that. Part of it is an artefact of how many candidates exist: the rank 1 share falls from 100% in 2-candidate events to 44.5% in events with nine or more. It stays far above the ~12% a random ordering would give, so the effect is real — but smaller than the raw 52% suggests.

Second place is not a near miss

The correct cluster scores a median of 0.556 of the winner’s TRKPTZ value — barely half. Only 10.7% come within 10% of the winner. It is second, and it is second by a wide margin, so this is not an ordering that is nearly right and needs a nudge.

The z term is anti-informative, not merely useless

At 110 vertices per event the pileup population is denser in z than the hard-scatter cluster’s own spread. There is always a pileup cluster sitting closer to the primary than the true one, so e−1.5|Δz| systematically rewards pileup. In 77.9% of recoverable failures the correct answer is the more distant cluster — the score is not mis-weighted, it is being applied to an axis that carries no separation left.

This is the same “merged pileup vertices” regime the WAVeS note describes, and it explains why the reconstructed-vertex features bought only +0.34 points: asking “is a pileup vertex claiming this z?” returns yes essentially always, so the question has little discriminating power left in it. It also says what does remain — timing is the only axis on which these two clusters genuinely differ, which is exactly what the 13.2-point time-quality term measures.

Failure is U-shaped in multiplicity

Total failure rate by forward reconstructed track count; the solid part is the no-candidate share, the faded part the recoverable share. Bin populations underneath.

The two ends fail for opposite reasons. Below two forward tracks, every single failure is a no-candidate failure — 58.9% fail and 58.9% have nothing to pick, so there is no selection decision being made at all. Above 20 tracks the no-candidate share collapses to 5.7% while total failure climbs back to 39.3%: the right cluster is nearly always present and is simply not chosen. The middle, around 6–12 tracks, is where the two effects cross and the overall rate is lowest.

Note that the crowded end is where Z+jets actually lives — 25,662 of 36,531 events carry 20 or more forward tracks. The dominant failure mode in this sample is therefore a pure selection failure in a track-rich environment, which is consistent with the rank distribution above and with the panels below.

Two-thirds of wrong picks are entirely pileup — not a marginal hard-scatter cluster mis-ordered, but a cluster with no hard-scatter content at all, chosen over one sitting 20 ps from truth. The last row is why the reconstructed pileup vertices were worth exporting: they separate these two populations, 37.8% against 23.8%. That is a real difference and a modest one, which matches what it buys in practice — +0.34 core-fraction points on Z+jets, and nothing at all on VBF or dijet, where this failure mode is rare.

And the events nobody can fix

30 events where no cluster is within 60 ps — 7.4% of Z+jets. No selector can recover these.

Why these matter for the input data

Across the recoverable failures, the median number of truth hard-scatter tracks in the best cluster of the event is 2, and the best purity available anywhere in the event exceeds 0.5 in only 39 of 100 events. In 25 of 100, the "correct" cluster itself has purity exactly zero — a pure pileup cluster that lands within 60 ps of the truth time by coincidence. In those events the training label is right by luck, not by physics.

Should the input data be re-evaluated?

Short answer: yes, and there is already an independent measurement pointing the same way.

The canonical-selection study measured Z+jets topology acceptance at 7.05% at μ=200 against 0.44% at μ=0 — a factor 16. Only about 6% of the Z+jets events in this selection would be there without pileup; the forward jet pair that admits them is supplied by pileup in roughly 94% of cases.

These panels are what that looks like event by event. The selection is admitting events whose forward region has essentially no hard-scatter content — median 2 HS tracks — and then asking a selector to find the hard-scatter vertex there. A quarter of the time the labelled answer is itself pileup that happened to have the right time.

What this implies, concretely

The 25.5-point Z+jets gap is partly a measurement of the selection, not of the model. Three things worth doing before any further modelling: require some minimum hard-scatter presence (e.g. ≥1 forward HS track, or a truth-matched forward jet) and re-measure the gap on that subset; report core fraction split by the number of HS tracks available, since the aggregate number mixes solvable and unsolvable events; and check whether the μ=0 control shows the same failures, which would separate a pileup effect from a reconstruction one.

None of this contradicts the earlier result that the information is present in the reco inputs — the pairwise probe still separates chosen from correct at 84%. It says the population being scored contains a large fraction of events where the question is close to ill-posed, and averaging over them is what produces 67%.
